# The shape of an answer, and how it gets broken

## Two minutes, weighted

| Part | Time | What it has to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Situation | 15s | where, when, what was at stake — enough context to care |
| Task | 10s | what *you* were responsible for, explicitly |
| Action | 60–70s | the sequence of things you did, with the decisions in it |
| Result | 20s | what changed, with a number or a concrete outcome |
| Reflection | 10s | what you would do differently |

Two minutes is short. Most people spend 90 seconds on the situation and run out
of time in the action — which is the only part being graded.

## The follow-up ladder every story must survive

Rehearse against these, in order. The story is ready when it holds at level 3.

1. **What did you personally do?** — separates you from the team
2. **Why that and not the alternative?** — tests whether it was a decision or a
   default
3. **What did the other people say / what pushed back?** — tests whether the
   situation was real
4. **How do you know it worked?** — tests the result
5. **What would you do differently?** — tests reflection

A story that dissolves at level 1 ("well, the team decided…") is not a story
about you.

## Tailoring one story to different competencies

The same migration can answer four questions. Change the first 20 seconds and
the emphasis, never the facts.

| Asked about | Lead with | Emphasise |
| --- | --- | --- |
| delivery | the deadline and what was at risk | sequencing, what you cut to land it |
| influence | the disagreement about the approach | how you changed someone's mind |
| failure | what went wrong and when you knew | detection, recovery, the fix that stuck |
| conflict | the other person's position, fairly stated | how it was resolved and what it cost |
| mentoring | the person, not the project | what they could do afterwards that they could not before |

Stating the other side fairly is the single strongest move in a conflict
story — it signals you understood it rather than won it.

## Anti-patterns, and what to do instead

- **"We" throughout.** Rewrite each action sentence to start with "I". Then add
  one honest sentence about what the team did — it reads as generous, not
  boastful.
- **The humble-brag failure.** "I cared too much about code quality" answers
  nothing. A real failure has a cost you can name. Pick one that was resolved,
  where the lesson is visible in how you work now.
- **The villain colleague.** Any story where the other person is simply wrong
  reads as a story about someone hard to work with. Give their position its best
  argument.
- **The hypothetical.** "I would usually…" is not an answer to "tell me about a
  time". If there is no instance, pick another competency and say so.
- **No outcome.** "And then we shipped it" — shipped what, and what changed?
- **Too old.** A senior candidate's best story should not be from their second
  job, unless nothing since compares, in which case say why.
- **Over-rehearsed.** Word-perfect delivery reads as recited. Rehearse the
  beats, not the sentences.

## When the user has no number

Common and fine. Options, in order of preference:

1. Go and find it — a dashboard, an old PR, a retro doc, a Slack thread.
2. A bounded estimate they can defend: "support tickets for that flow dropped to
   roughly a third; I remember because we stopped having a weekly triage for it."
3. A concrete non-numeric outcome: "the on-call rotation stopped being paged for
   it."

What is never an option is a number that came from the assistant.
