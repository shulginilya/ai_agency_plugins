# What the loop usually looks like

Archetypes, not rules. Use them to draft an inferred loop and to know which
question to ask the recruiter — never to tell the user what will happen.

## Big tech / large public company

`recruiter screen → technical phone screen → onsite loop (4–6 back to back)`

The loop is standardised and the interviewers are calibrated against a written
rubric they will not show you. Expect two algorithmic coding rounds, one or two
system design rounds, and a behavioural round run against published values.
Feedback goes to a hiring committee, not to the manager, so a strong manager
conversation does not rescue a weak coding round.

Prepare: data structures under time pressure, a design round that starts from
requirements clarification, and behavioural answers that survive three levels of
"and what did *you* do?".

## Scale-up (Series A–C), the common Berlin case

`recruiter screen → hiring manager → technical (take-home or live pairing) →
system/architecture discussion → founder or values round`

Three to five stages over two to four weeks. Interviewers are the team, mostly
uncalibrated, and the questions come from the work they did last quarter — which
is why the job description's stack is a much better predictor here than at a big
company. The final round is often with a founder or a VP and is genuinely about
whether you raise the team's level.

Prepare: their actual stack, a take-home you can defend line by line, and an
opinion about how you would work in a team of their size.

## Early startup / founding engineer

`founder call → paid trial or take-home → team day or pairing session → offer`

Fast, personal, and badly structured on purpose. The bar is breadth and speed of
judgement, not depth in one area. Compensation is usually negotiated by the
founder in the first call, so the number comes up earlier than anywhere else.

Prepare: a clear story about what you would own, a position on shipping versus
correctness, and your number.

## Agency / consultancy

`recruiter screen → technical interview → client-facing simulation → commercial`

Two bars: can you build it, and can you be put in front of a client. Expect a
scenario round — "the client wants X by Friday and it cannot be done" — and
questions about estimation, scope and how you explain a technical constraint to
a non-technical stakeholder.

Prepare: stories about scope and stakeholders, and an estimate you defended.

## Enterprise / German Mittelstand

`HR screen → Fachgespräch (technical, with the team lead) → second round with HR
and the department head → contract`

Slower, more formal, more paperwork. HR runs the first and often the last stage
and cares about availability, notice period and salary expectation in a way the
technical stages do not. The technical round is a conversation about your
experience more often than a live exercise.

Prepare: a chronological career narrative, your `Gehaltsvorstellung` as a range
you can justify, your notice period, and a translated version of your best
project story.

## Freelance / contract client

`intro call with the client or a broker → technical deep-dive with the team →
rate and terms`

One or two conversations, days not weeks. The technical stage is about
derisking: can you start next week and not need managing. Nobody asks you to
invert a binary tree. They ask what you shipped, what broke, and who you worked
with.

Prepare: two project stories with outcomes, your day rate, availability and
notice, and references.

## Berlin and EU specifics worth knowing

- **Probearbeit / trial day** is common at product companies and should be paid.
  An unpaid multi-day trial is a signal, not an opportunity.
- **Gehaltsvorstellung** is asked early and often in writing. A range with a
  justification travels better than a single number.
- **Notice periods** (often 1–3 months) shape the whole schedule; an employer
  who ignores yours in planning is telling you something.
- **English vs German**: most product companies interview in English; enterprise
  and Mittelstand often do the HR stages in German. Ask which, explicitly.
- **EU-remote contracts** add a compliance stage — entity, invoicing, VAT,
  sometimes a background check. It is not an interview round but it takes time.

## The message that replaces a week of guessing

When the process is not stated, the first deliverable is this, sent to the
recruiter today:

> Thanks — before I put time into preparing, could you tell me what the process
> looks like? Specifically: how many stages, what format each one is (live
> coding, take-home, design discussion), who I would be speaking to, and roughly
> what timeline you are working to.

Recruiters answer this almost always, and the answer converts three inferred
rounds into three stated ones.
